Leather Jacket Grades Explained: A-Grade, AB-Grade, Full-Grain and Lambskin

By Safe-T TECH International, Sialkot, Pakistan · Published 31 October 2026

← All guides

Ask three suppliers what grade of leather they use and you will often get three different answers — and sometimes three different jackets. As a leather jacket manufacturer in Sialkot, Pakistan, we work with every grade described in this guide, and the questions we hear most from new brands are always the same: what does each grade actually mean, how does it feel and age, and which one should I choose for my price point? This guide answers those questions in plain language, so you can specify your leather with confidence instead of taking a supplier's word for it.

What a leather "grade" actually describes

A grade is a shorthand for the quality of the hide and how much of its natural surface survives into the finished jacket. Every animal skin carries marks of its life — scars, insect bites, stretch marks, variations in thickness. The cleanest, most even hides are graded highest; hides with more visible marks are graded lower and are usually corrected — sanded, embossed or heavily finished — to hide the imperfections. Grading is therefore not about whether the leather is "real" or not; it is about surface quality, consistency and how the material behaves over years of wear. Two jackets can both be genuine leather and still be worlds apart in look, feel and lifespan, and the grade is usually the reason why.

One caution before we go further: there is no single worldwide grading law. Terms like A-Grade and AB-Grade are trade practice, and one supplier's A-Grade may be another's AB. That is why this guide describes what you should feel and see in each grade — and why, at the end, we explain how to lock the grade down in writing before production starts.

Full-grain leather: the top of the range

Full-grain is the outermost layer of the hide, used exactly as nature made it — nothing sanded off, nothing embossed over it. Because the grain layer is intact, full-grain is the strongest and most breathable form of leather, and it is the only one that develops a true patina: the rich, slightly darkened character that makes a ten-year-old jacket look better than a new one. The trade-off is honesty. Full-grain shows every natural mark, so only a small share of hides qualify, and the jackets cost more. Choose full-grain when your brand sells heritage, durability and a jacket that improves with age — biker jackets, classic bombers and premium fashion styles. If your customer wants a flawless, uniform surface, full-grain will disappoint them; if they want character, nothing else comes close.

A-Grade leather: the clean, dependable standard

A-Grade is the workhorse grade for quality jacket production. These are clean hides with minimal natural marks, a consistent grain and an even hand feel across the whole skin. The surface may be lightly finished to even out tone, but the grain itself is genuine and uncorrected. For most brands, A-Grade is the sweet spot: it looks premium, wears well, holds its shape and colour, and keeps the jacket at a price your customer can actually pay. The majority of the jackets we make for fashion and streetwear labels — biker, bomber and fashion styles alike — are cut from A-Grade leather, because it delivers the best balance of appearance, durability and cost. If you are launching a leather line and unsure where to start, start here.

AB-Grade leather: the smart value choice

AB-Grade hides carry more visible natural marks — healed scars, uneven grain or small blemishes — and are corrected to make them usable: the surface is lightly sanded and re-grained or finished so the marks no longer show. The result is a perfectly presentable jacket at a noticeably lower material cost, which is why AB-Grade suits high-volume or price-sensitive ranges. What you give up is some breathability and some of the ageing character: corrected surfaces do not develop a patina the way full-grain does, and heavy correction can make a jacket feel slightly stiffer at first. AB-Grade is a sensible choice when your market competes on price and your customer judges the jacket on style and fit first — just make sure your supplier tells you the leather is corrected, so you are pricing and describing it honestly.

Lambskin: softness above all

Lambskin is prized for one thing above everything else: softness. It is thin, light and buttery to the touch, and it drapes like fabric, which is why it dominates women's fashion jackets, slim biker styles and lightweight fashion pieces. That softness comes with a trade-off in toughness — lambskin scratches and scuffs more easily than cowhide, and it is not the right pick for a hard-wearing motorcycle jacket. Think of it as a fashion material rather than a workhorse: perfect when the jacket's job is to look and feel luxurious, less suited to daily rough use. Lambskin is usually offered in A-Grade quality, and a well-made lambskin jacket should feel almost weightless when you pick it up.

Premium calfskin: the refined middle ground

Calfskin sits between lambskin and cowhide: finer-grained and softer than standard cowhide, but denser and more durable than lambskin. Premium calfskin has a tight, smooth grain that takes dye beautifully, giving deep, even colours — a real advantage for fashion colours beyond black and brown. It is the grade we recommend when a brand wants a refined, dressy jacket that still holds up to regular wear: tailored fashion jackets, smart bombers and premium retail styles. Like lambskin, it rewards good construction — clean stitching, quality linings and solid hardware matter more on a fine-grained leather, because there is nowhere for poor workmanship to hide.

The grades side by side

  • Full-grain: strongest, most breathable, develops a patina; natural marks visible; highest cost.
  • A-Grade: clean, consistent, genuine grain; best balance of quality and price; the default for most brands.
  • AB-Grade: corrected surface hides natural marks; lowest cost; less breathability and no real patina.
  • Lambskin: exceptionally soft and light; ideal for fashion; scratches more easily.
  • Premium calfskin: fine, smooth grain with rich colour; refined look with good durability.

Notice that no grade is "bad" — the mistake is using the wrong grade for the wrong jacket. A corrected AB-Grade biker jacket at a fair price is a good product; the same jacket sold as full-grain would be a dishonest one.

How to judge a leather jacket before you order

Whether you are checking a sample or a supplier's showroom piece, judge the same five things:

  • Touch and drape: does it feel like skin — supple, with a little give — or like coated board? Stiffness that never softens is a warning sign.
  • Grain: look closely. Real grain is irregular; a perfectly repeated pattern usually means heavy embossing over a corrected surface.
  • Edges and stitching: clean, even stitching and finished edges show in-house care; loose threads and raw, fraying edges do not.
  • Lining and hardware: a good jacket has a lining chosen for the style — quilted, satin or custom — and zips and snaps that move smoothly and feel solid. Trims and hardware should be specified, not left to chance.
  • Smell: genuine leather smells like leather, not chemicals. A strong plastic or solvent smell suggests heavy finishing or worse.

Ask the supplier to name the grade, the animal and the finishing in writing. "Genuine leather" alone is not a specification — it is a label that tells you almost nothing.

Mistakes worth avoiding

Three errors come up again and again. First, choosing a grade by price alone, then wondering why the jacket does not match the brand's image. Second, accepting "genuine leather" as a specification and discovering the grade only when customers complain. Third, skipping the sample: leather varies from hide to hide, and a sample is the only way to confirm that the grade, colour and hand feel you specified are the ones you will actually receive. A manufacturer who resists any of these checks is telling you something — listen to it.
